The University Rover Challenge is a Mars-analogue competition. One of its tasks asks a rover to do something that sounds like a joke and isn't: find a keyboard and type on it, without a human. From December 2024 to May 2025 I worked on that task with BRACU Mongol-Tori's autonomous sub-team.

The task decomposes into three problems that each look easy alone: *where's the keyboard*, *where's each key*, and *how do I put the arm's tip there*. None of them is easy while a rover is parked on uneven ground.

## Where's each key

I built a standalone, lightweight vision unit for alignment and key detection, deliberately separate from the rover's main computer, so typing couldn't starve navigation and navigation couldn't stall typing. Custom OCR and image processing told the keys apart and located each one.

<Fig src="/media/lab/detect-keyboard.webp" alt="A keyboard with individual keys detected and boxed" caption="Keyboard and individual keys, detected for the typing task." contain />

I also ran the dataset pipeline for URC 2025: the labelled images that taught the detector what a key looks like at rover distance and at an angle.

## Where's the tip

The arm had two joints that mattered for reaching. A two-link arm has a closed-form inverse: given where the tip should go, the elbow angle comes from the law of cosines and the shoulder from two arctangents.

An in-house GUI and simulation environment made calibration possible before the hardware existed. A laptop is the cheapest place to find a failure.

You can drive the arm in [the ground arena](/#ground): click a key, or use the arrow keys, and it solves for the joint angles live. Push it to the far corner of the keyboard and it tells you it can't reach, with the distance.
