The localisation filter had 21 tests, and every one checked a *property*, not a trajectory: the exponential/log round-trip, covariance symmetry, positive-definiteness, the sign of one update. None of them drove the filter along a path and asked how far it had drifted from where it actually was.

Because there was no *actually*. Comparing two estimators only tells you whether they agree with each other. It can't rank them. You need a case where the true position is known by construction.

## Build the truth

A Fossen-model plant (the standard rigid-body-plus-hydrodynamics model for marine vehicles) produces exact position, attitude and velocity every tick. So the harness can synthesise IMU readings, optical-flow velocity, depth and heading at their real measured rates, and score the filter's drift against ground truth instead of its own previous output.

<Fig src="/media/mongla/sim-bluerov-gate.webp" alt="A simulated vehicle near a gate in the simulator" caption="Simulation's real job here isn't pretty water. It's a world where the answer is known, so an estimate can be wrong in a measurable way." wide />

## The frame seam that cost −1,988 degrees

One convention had to be pinned rather than assumed. The filter's world is NED (north-east-down, z pointing down) with an FRD body frame (forward-right-down). The plant is x-forward, y-starboard, z-down. Same convention, nothing to remap. That's worth saying out loud, because a frame seam is exactly what produced a **−1,988° yaw runaway** the first time the thrust allocator met the plant with an unpinned assumption about which frame it was reading.

## The first numbers that mean what they say

Sixty seconds of a yawing trajectory with full aiding:

They're not impressive in isolation; simulation is generous. They matter because they're the first numbers in the project's history that mean what they appear to mean: an estimate compared to a truth, on a path complex enough that agreement wasn't guaranteed by construction.
