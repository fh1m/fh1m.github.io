A Doppler velocity log bounces sound off the seabed and reads your speed over ground from the echo's frequency shift. It's the instrument underwater navigation is built on, and it costs more than everything else on a student vehicle put together. Mongla doesn't have one.

What it does have is a camera pointed at the floor. When the vehicle moves, the floor slides across the image. The question is how to turn pixels per second into metres per second.

## One ratio

A floor feature directly below the camera, at height *h*, moving at speed *v*, slides across the sensor at <code>v · f / h</code> pixels per second, where *f* is the focal length in pixels. Turn it around and pixel motion is a speed, as long as you know *h*.

That's the whole sensor. Notice what it hides: *h* appears as a ratio, so an error in height becomes the same percentage error in every speed, in the same direction, forever. Drag the numbers and watch the dead-reckoning drift that a small height error buys:

## Checking it against a tape

On 7 September 2026 the downward camera went on a bench rig at 0.72 m above the floor (by tape), and was slid 30 cm by hand along each axis.

| slide | measured | error | height it implies |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| lateral | 30.13 cm | +0.13 | 0.72 m |
| forward | 31.09 cm | +1.09 | 0.69 m |
| back | 31.04 cm | +1.04 | 0.70 m |
| **tape** | — | — | **0.72 m** |

Worst error 1.09 cm on 30 cm, 3.6 %. The raw file holds a fourth run, a deliberately diagonal slide that read 42.53 cm at −171.8°: not a 30 cm test, but a check that the angle comes out right, and it did. The strongest result is the last column: each slide recovers the tape's height to within 3 cm, so the scale chain closes on itself.

For scale: a commercial DVL's bottom track is quoted at 0.5–1 %, and published monocular visual odometry in real turbid water at 0.89–1.88 %. This was in air, on a hand slide whose own precision is about ±1 cm, so it's an upper bound on the sensor's error, not a measurement of it.

## Three bugs, one mistake

Before those numbers, the capture tool reported a correct sensor as *short* three times in a row. The live trace showed the slide tracked correctly each time; the capture threw the measurement away.

1. **The end-of-move check lived inside `if v.ok:`.** A move ends with the rig stopping, a stopped rig produces refusals, so the stillness that defines the end was exactly when the check was skipped. Traced 29.89 cm, logged nothing.
2. **The capture started at first motion**, dropping the slow start and stop. Traced 30.3 and 30.5 cm; scored 19.6, 26.3 and 21.6.
3. **Stillness was judged on instantaneous velocity**, and a refused interval read as zero speed, so a burst of refusals mid-slide looked like stopping. Traced 24.9 and 30.0 cm; scored 12.1 and 8.5.

<Pull>All three were the same mistake in different clothes: treating "I could not measure" as "it did not move."</Pull>

The fix that ended it wasn't a better heuristic. It was a button. The operator knows when they stopped, and no window short enough to be responsive can tell a slow hand from a still rig.

## What it still isn't

Every number above was taken in air. Through water the focal length changes (measured: 741 px against 513.94 in air, a ratio of 1.44; see [the flat port](/notes/one-lens-two-fields-of-view) for why water changes it at all), and the rectified water path has produced no measured velocity yet. The project calls it verified as arithmetic, not as a sensor, and keeps that line in the ledger until a slide runs through it.
