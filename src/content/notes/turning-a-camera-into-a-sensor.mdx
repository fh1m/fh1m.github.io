A bottom-lock Doppler velocity log reads speed relative to the seabed from acoustic frequency shifts. It provides a velocity reference where GPS cannot reach—not an absolute position fix. Mongla's low-cost design does not include one.

What it does have is a camera pointed at the floor. When the vehicle moves, the floor slides across the image. The question is how to turn pixels per second into metres per second.

## One ratio

A floor feature directly below the camera, at height *h*, moving at speed *v*, slides across the sensor at <code>v · f / h</code> pixels per second, where *f* is the focal length in pixels. Turn it around and pixel motion is a speed, as long as you know *h*.

That's the scale dependence the simple model isolates: with fixed calibration and a flat floor, multiplying the believed height by a factor multiplies the reported horizontal velocity by the same factor. Drift also depends on speed and elapsed time. Drag the numbers to explore that relationship; rotation, vertical motion and feature-tracking errors are outside this model:

## Checking it against a tape

On 7 September 2026 the downward camera went on a bench rig at 0.72 m above the floor (by tape), and was slid 30 cm by hand along each axis.

<FlowRecords />

For the **three nominally axial 30 cm trials—lateral, forward and back—** the largest error in the **integrated distance magnitude** is 1.09 cm, or about 3.6%. That is a statement about these trials, not a general error bound on the velocity sensor or its two-dimensional trajectory.

The raw JSON also preserves a fourth record: magnitude 42.53 cm, angle −171.8°, and 31 fallback samples. The accompanying measurement ledger describes that run as a deliberately diagonal diagnostic. Its JSON still contains the logger's nominal `truth_m: 0.3`, so that field alone does not establish the actual diagonal travel distance. I do not include it in the three-axis summary or treat it as independently validated directional accuracy.

Direction needs a separate reference. The forward trial includes a 6.93 cm cross-axis component in the reported coordinate frame. The ledger attributes the angle to the hand path; without an independently tracked two-dimensional path, this experiment cannot separate operator direction from directional estimation error.

The implied-height column is calculated as `assumed height × tape travel / measured distance`. For fixed tracked image displacements under the linear projection used here, measured distance scales with assumed height, so that assumed height cancels algebraically: the column re-expresses the same image displacement using the known travel. Comparing it with the lens-to-floor tape measurement is a useful consistency check, not a second independent experiment.

A DVL's velocity specification and a visual-odometry paper's trajectory error are not the same statistic as maximum distance-magnitude error over three short hand slides. These trials were in air; the ledger describes the manual reference as roughly ±1 cm, without an independently calibrated motion rig. That uncertainty does not establish an upper bound on the algorithm's error, because reference and estimation errors can reinforce or cancel. The linked [turbid-water visual-odometry study](https://doi.org/10.3390/s19030687) is further reading, not a like-for-like benchmark won by this test.

## Three bugs, one mistake

Before those numbers, the capture tool reported a correct sensor as *short* three times in a row. The live trace showed the slide tracked correctly each time; the capture threw the measurement away.

1. **The end-of-move check lived inside `if v.ok:`.** A move ends with the rig stopping, a stopped rig produces refusals, so the stillness that defines the end was exactly when the check was skipped. Traced 29.89 cm, logged nothing.
2. **The capture started at first motion**, dropping the slow start and stop. Traced 30.3 and 30.5 cm; scored 19.6, 26.3 and 21.6.
3. **Stillness was judged on instantaneous velocity**, and a refused interval read as zero speed, so a burst of refusals mid-slide looked like stopping. Traced 24.9 and 30.0 cm; scored 12.1 and 8.5.

<Pull>All three were the same mistake in different clothes: treating "I could not measure" as "it did not move."</Pull>

The fix that ended it wasn't a better heuristic. It was a button. The operator knows when they stopped, and no window short enough to be responsive can tell a slow hand from a still rig.

## What it still isn't

Every number above was taken in air. Through water the focal length changes (measured: 741 px against 513.94 in air, a ratio of 1.44; see [the flat port](/notes/one-lens-two-fields-of-view) for why water changes it at all), and the rectified water path has produced no measured velocity yet. The project calls it verified as arithmetic, not as a sensor, and keeps that line in the ledger until a slide runs through it.
