A Doppler velocity log is the obvious way to measure an underwater vehicle's speed over the ground. It's also expensive, and we didn't have one. So the question became: what do we already have that can measure motion?

The downward camera. If you know the height above the floor and the camera's focal length, then optical flow (how far the floor's texture slides between two frames) *is* a velocity:

The geometry is simple. Height is the term that ruins you: twice as high, half the pixel motion for the same speed. Get it wrong and every velocity, and every metre of dead reckoning built on it, is wrong by the same factor in the same direction.

## The verification that actually worked

Three real 30 cm slides on a bench rig, downward camera at 0.72 m, focal length 513.94 px. The honest column is the last one: the height each measurement *implies*, recovered from the slide it got wrong.

| slide | measured | error | implied height |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| lateral 30 cm | 30.13 cm | +0.13 | 0.72 m |
| forward 30 cm | 31.09 cm | +1.09 | 0.69 m |
| back 30 cm | 31.04 cm | +1.04 | 0.70 m |
| tape says | — | — | **0.72 m** |

Worst error: 1.09 cm on 30 cm, about 3.6 %. That's several times worse than even a cheap DVL's quoted ~1 %, and the same order as published monocular visual odometry in turbid water. It was measured in air, on a hand slide whose own precision is about ±1 cm, so it's an upper bound on the error, not a measurement of it. The page says so.

<Pull>A number with no bar can't be wrong, so it never gets checked. It just gets quoted, and eventually shipped.</Pull>

What makes this trustworthy isn't that the estimator agreed with another estimator; that only measures agreement. It's that the truth was known: a tape on the floor, a slide of fixed length. Construct the case where you already know the answer, then see if the machine finds it.
