Underwater there's no radio, no GPS, and no second chance to explain yourself. This one physical fact writes most of an underwater vehicle's architecture for you.

Seawater conducts. A radio wave entering a conductor induces currents that eat its energy, and it decays exponentially with depth. The distance over which it falls to about 37 % is the *skin depth*, and it shrinks with frequency.

That's why submarines talk at very low frequencies through kilometre-long antennas, and why a student vehicle the size of a suitcase gets nothing at all. GPS is off the table a hand's width under the surface. So is radar, so is a telemetry link, so is a human with a joystick.

## What it forces

If you can't get a position fix from the sky, you estimate it from the inside: dead reckoning on an IMU, corrected by whatever you can measure locally. Depth from pressure. Velocity from a [camera pointed at the floor](/notes/turning-a-camera-into-a-sensor). Heading from the attitude estimator. If you can't stream telemetry home, the vehicle has to be right on its own, and has to log enough to explain itself afterwards.

<Fig src="/media/mongla-docs/pool-top.webp" alt="An underwater vehicle in a pool seen from above, a tether trailing" caption="From the surface, everything looks reachable. A few millimetres down, the radio disagrees." />

Sound travels well underwater, which is why sonar and acoustic modems exist. But sound is slow (about 1.5 km/s) and its bandwidth is narrow. It's a way to whisper, not a way to steer.

<Pull>A vehicle either understood what it was looking at, or it didn't, and it won't tell you which.</Pull>

That's why the interesting engineering isn't the happy path. It's what the vehicle does when it *can't* do something. On a well-built stack the answer is never a silent workaround that keeps the demo running. It's a loud refusal and a written reason, because nobody is going to be on the radio to ask.
