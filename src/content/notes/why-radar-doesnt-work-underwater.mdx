Start with the thing you lose. On land, a robot that doesn't know where it is asks the sky: four satellites, a few nanoseconds of timing, and it knows its position to a few metres. If it gets confused, someone on a laptop can see its telemetry and take the controls.

Put the same robot one hand's width under the sea and every one of those options disappears. Not because the electronics fail. Because the water is a conductor.

## Why a conductor eats radio

A radio wave is an electric field and a magnetic field chasing each other through space. In a vacuum nothing resists them. In salt water the electric field pushes on dissolved ions, the ions move, moving charge is a current, and the current turns the wave's energy into heat. The wave doesn't bounce off: it's absorbed, exponentially, a fixed fraction per metre.

How big a fraction depends on two things: how well the water conducts, and how fast the field is wiggling. Faster wiggles do more damage per metre. Drag the frequency and the water type below and watch where the signal dies.

The numbers it produces aren't close calls. GPS at 1.575 GHz in seawater is gone within about five centimetres: the water takes roughly 8 dB every centimetre, and a good receiver only has 38.5 dB to spare. Even the very low frequencies navies use to reach submarines, tens of kilohertz from antennas kilometres long, only reach a few tens of metres down. A suitcase-sized student vehicle gets nothing at all.

Fresh water is kinder (a pool conducts hundreds of times less than the sea), which is why a vehicle tethered in a pool can feel like it has a link. Switch the explorable to the pool and you'll see why that feeling doesn't survive the trip to open water.

## What it forces

Take away the sky, the link and the human, and the design writes itself:

- **Position has to come from the inside.** An IMU integrates acceleration into velocity and velocity into position, and it drifts, because integrating noise accumulates it. So everything local that can be measured gets fused in to pull it back: depth from pressure, [velocity from a camera pointed at the floor](/notes/turning-a-camera-into-a-sensor), heading from the attitude estimator, and occasional fixes from recognising something.
- **The vehicle has to be right on its own.** There's no one to notice it's confused, so it must notice itself, and say so in its logs.
- **The reflexes can't depend on the thinking.** If the computer that runs the neural network stalls, the loop that holds depth must not. That's why [Mongla](/notes/mongla) keeps its 500 Hz control loop on a separate board.

## What does get through

Sound. Water carries it beautifully: about 1,500 m/s, four times faster than in air, over kilometres. That's why acoustic modems and sonar exist. But sound is slow next to light and its bandwidth is narrow, so it's a way to whisper a few bytes, not a way to steer. And blue-green light travels tens of metres in clear water, which is why the next part of this series is about what water does to light.

<Pull>A vehicle either understood what it was looking at, or it didn't, and it won't tell you which.</Pull>

So the interesting engineering isn't the happy path. It's what the vehicle does when it *can't* do something, with nobody on the radio to ask. On a well-built stack the answer is never a silent workaround that keeps the demo running. It's a loud refusal and a written reason.
