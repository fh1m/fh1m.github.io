A detector that looks healthy can be steering by the past. The boxes appear, the counters tick up, every node reports green, and the frame it's looking at is a third of a second old. For a vehicle moving at half a metre per second, that's a target that moved 17 cm while you weren't watching.

This note is about where that third of a second comes from. The answer turned out to invert the advice in essentially every reference on the problem.

## What everybody assumes

Linux cameras speak V4L2. The driver owns a small ring of buffers; the camera fills them, your program takes one, reads it, and gives it back. If your program is slow, the buffers fill up. The usual mental picture is a queue of *recent* frames: if you fall behind, drain the queue and take the last one, and you're caught up.

That picture is wrong in one specific way. Stall a reader for 400 ms at 60 fps and read the driver's own sequence counter off what's waiting:

<Explore id="fossil" n="fig 1" title="What a full camera queue actually holds" hint="drag the stall and pick a policy" caption="The driver owns four buffers. While the reader stalls, the camera fills the free ones and then has nowhere to put anything, so every later frame is dropped before it exists. Measured on two cameras from different vendors, so it's the V4L2/UVC contract, not one driver." />

The queue holds frames **+1 to +4**: the four frames from the *start* of the stall, aged 396, 380, 364 and 344 ms. Frames +5 to +24 were never captured at all. The kernel had no free buffer, so the driver dropped them on the floor.

<Pull>The queue is not a backlog of recent frames. It is a fossil record of the moment the consumer stalled.</Pull>

## Four policies, one stall

Same camera, same 400 ms stall, ten repetitions each:

<Bars title="age of the frame acted on after a 400 ms stall" unit=" ms" rows={[

**B** recovers 12 % of the staleness, because draining a fossil record gets you the least-old fossil. **C** gets a fresh frame but makes the caller wait for it. **D** is a small thread whose only job is to dequeue and immediately requeue, so the driver *always* has a free buffer, never drops a frame, and the newest one is always about one frame old. Under a busy consumer (25 ms of work per tick, the shape of an inference) it holds 22.4 ms median and 31.95 ms at the 95th percentile, at the camera's full 62.5 Hz with zero sequence gaps.

## Freshness over throughput

For a moving vehicle, an old correct answer is a wrong answer. Throughput is a benchmark number; freshness is what keeps a control loop honest. The keep-up thread throws work away on purpose, and that's the right trade.

<Pull by="John Carmack">The speed of light sucks.</Pull>

Push it to the end and the goal stops being "fast". It's to make the software vanish as a source of delay, so that what's left is the camera itself. On Mongla's live hardware, both cameras, that's where it ended:

The lesson generalises past cameras: any time a slow stage sits behind a fast one, ask what happens to the backlog. If the answer is *it queues*, you've built a time machine pointed the wrong way. [Dristy](/notes/dristy) has the same rule printed on its README, and the 2024 version of me met the problem without a name for it, [narrating a bottle](/notes/half-a-second) half a second late.
