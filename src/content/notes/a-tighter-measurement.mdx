The question was supposed to be simple: does *when* a landmark fix arrives matter for a known localisation defect? With optical flow healthy, the answer was clean. A 2-second resection window at t = 0, t = 20 or t = 55 s all landed in the same 0.20–0.28 m band.

That falls straight out of the defect's mechanism. If dead-reckoning error isn't growing between fixes, a late fix is worth exactly what an early one is. Timing wasn't the variable. Count was.

One resection anywhere captures much of the benefit, and every fix after that averages the estimate down rather than correcting a particular moment. For mission design that's a reframe: it doesn't matter *where* the vehicle sights a recognisable landmark. It matters that it keeps sighting one for *longer*.

<Pull>The previous commit said the opposite, in my own words: "a fix early is worth far more than a fix often." That claim is retracted here.</Pull>

It was an inference, not a measurement, and the measurement disagreed. The retraction is the content of this entry, not a footnote to it. Two commits from the same author, about the same defect, on consecutive days: one wrong, one measured. Nothing in the codebase changed between them except which claim was allowed to keep standing.
