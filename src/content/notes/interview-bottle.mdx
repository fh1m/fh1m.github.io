The screenshots are dated 3 December 2023. They come from my object-detection task for BRACU Duburi: take a water-bottle dataset, label it, train a detector, and show it working. I used Kaggle photographs, LabelImg and SSD MobileNet v2 through the TensorFlow Object Detection API. The original README says I “followed all the instructions,” then shows seven screenshots.

Nearly three years later, the workspace was still there: TensorBoard event files, TFRecords, checkpoints and an exported model. That is much more useful than a screenshot on its own. The image records what looked convincing; the files let the experiment be questioned.

This re-analysis runs the preserved model, not a replacement trained with hindsight. The current replay reproduces the published scores. It also exposes details that the short version of the story had blurred: the model configuration's actual image size, the evaluator protocol, and the difference between a plausible diagnosis and a demonstrated fix.

## A model name is not its configuration

The old README calls the model **SSD MobileNet v2 320×320**. The upstream TensorFlow recipe has that name too. Its fixed-shape resizer, and both preserved training and export configurations, actually specify **300×300**. The recipe name is not being used here as evidence of a different declared input size.

SSD predicts object scores and box offsets around default boxes at several feature-map scales. MobileNet supplies the feature extractor. The preserved configuration has six anchor layers, a one-class head, a focal classification loss and a smooth-L1 localization loss. A class score and a well-fitted rectangle are related training objectives, not the same output.

That is why “Bottle: 0.88” is not an overlap score. It does not say that the predicted rectangle agrees with the annotation by 88%, nor establish an 88% chance that the whole detection is correct. The attractive number in the screenshot is not a substitute for evaluating its box.

## What the logs remember—and what they do not

<Fig src="/media/notes/interview-bottle-training_2023.webp" alt="Training losses and learning rate read back from the 2023 event files: learning rate warms up to 0.8; regularisation loss jumps and stays high" caption="Read back from the 2023 event files. The learning rate climbs to 0.8, and the regularisation loss jumps with it." wide plot />

- **15 training images and 10 validation images** in the preserved records. The additional 15-image file called “test” does not add 15 independent examples.
- **4,200 steps in the top-level loss trace**, against 50,000 configured. A later nested `train/train` trace reaches 1,200 steps. These are surviving attempts, not a single clean experiment manifest proving every step that ran.
- **A schedule copied across a large change in batch size.** The upstream recipe pairs a base learning rate of 0.8 with a batch of 512 and eight replicas. My configuration uses a batch of eight while retaining the schedule and replica settings.

Applying the linear batch-scaling heuristic gives `0.8 × 8 / 512 = 0.0125`. That is a candidate to investigate, not proof that every update was exactly 64 times too large. The rule was established in a particular large-batch training setting; fine-tuning, normalization, momentum and the data can change what works.

The actual replay reads a final logged learning rate of 0.796 and regularization loss rising from about 0.320 to 0.934. Those are observations. Their timing makes the inherited schedule worth investigating, but it does not isolate the learning rate as the cause of the validation result. A controlled retraining comparison has not been performed.

The complete contact sheet is more useful than one selected overlay. Validation includes a bottle lying horizontally. That is a condition worth examining—not an isolated rotation experiment establishing what caused one error.

## The grade

The re-grade's first finding came before any model ran:

<Pull>test.tfrecord is a byte-for-byte copy of train.tfrecord.</Pull>

All 15 “test” images also occur in training. The current audit confirms identical TFRecord file hashes and identical decoded pixels. The model scores AP@0.5 = 1.000 on that file. That is in-sample performance, not a generalization test; the number alone does not prove which internal shortcut the network learned.

The ten validation images have no exact decoded-pixel matches in the training set. That makes them a better held-out check, though exact deduplication does not establish different scenes, products or acquisition conditions.

| Evaluation record | AP@0.5 | What it establishes |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Re-grade on the duplicated “test” file | 1.000 | Performance on training images |
| Re-grade on the ten validation images | 0.405 | Held-out image performance under the current evaluator |
| Historical evaluation log at step 4000 | 0.506 | A recorded COCO-evaluator result, not an interchangeable rerun |

The distinction in the final row matters. The preserved training configuration selects the TensorFlow Object Detection API's COCO metrics. The re-grade computes **VOC-style all-point interpolated AP at IoU 0.5**. A checkpoint called `ckpt-5` carries training step 4000, while the exported checkpoint has no step variable. Without reconciling the exact evaluated weights and protocol, the difference between 0.506 and 0.405 is not a measured deterioration over time.

## The box is allowed to be wrong

The dry-image coverage audit explains why the screenshot deserves a closer look:

| Box population | Median share of image area |
| --- | ---: |
| Top predicted box on validation images | 72% |
| Largest annotated bottle box on validation images | 31% |
| Largest annotated bottle box on training images | 51% |

These are separate medians, not the overlap of a particular pair of boxes. They are consistent with a broad-box or framing bias. They do not, by themselves, prove that the network solved the task by memorizing a central rectangle.

Intersection over union gives a concrete reason to care about the extra background:

```text
IoU = area(prediction ∩ annotation)
      ÷ area(prediction ∪ annotation)
```

Consider an illustrative case where a 31%-area annotation lies entirely inside a 72%-area prediction. The intersection is 31% and the union 72%, giving an IoU of about **0.431**. The real `iou` function reproduces that calculation on constructed boxes. This is not a newly measured median IoU for the dataset; it is geometry showing how a box can contain the bottle and still fail a 0.5 overlap requirement.

There are also several different uses of IoU in this pipeline:

| Stage | Preserved threshold | What is compared |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Training anchor matcher | 0.5 | Default boxes and annotations |
| Non-maximum suppression | 0.6 | Competing predicted boxes |
| Re-grade match acceptance | 0.5 | A prediction and an annotation |

The training matcher additionally forces a match for each annotated object, so its threshold is not the complete assignment rule. NMS removes competing predictions; it does not compare them with ground truth and cannot certify that the surviving rectangle is accurate.

## What AP counts that a screenshot does not

The re-grade collects detections down to a score of 0.01, sorts them by score and matches each accepted prediction to an annotation at IoU 0.5. A matched object cannot be counted again. Cumulative true and false positives form a precision–recall curve; the evaluator integrates its interpolated envelope.

A second box on an already matched bottle can therefore be a false positive, even if it looks plausible. A missing low-scoring bottle costs recall. A high-scoring but oversized box can be wrong before any later correct box is considered.

The illustrative dry/wet image sheet uses a different display cutoff: it draws the top box only above 0.3. That is a visualization choice, not the AP evaluator's inclusion threshold. Looking at the visible boxes alone does not reconstruct the metric.

The annotated training boxes cover a larger median fraction of the frame than the validation boxes in this audit. That makes scale and position diversity worth testing; it is not a controlled isolation of composition or rotation. A detector that works on large centred bottles has not thereby learned to find a small, partly hidden object elsewhere. The proposed padding and augmentation changes target that hypothesis; their benefit still needs a new experiment.

## Would it have survived the pool?

The task belonged to an underwater team, so the next question concerns the image domain. The validation photographs pass through the same defined RGB attenuation-and-veil transform used in [Green, at depth](/notes/green-at-depth/):

```text
I(channel, d) = J(channel) × exp(−k(channel) × d)
                + A(channel) × (1 − exp(−k(channel) × d))
```

`J` is the input image, `k` a per-channel attenuation coefficient, `A` the veil colour and `d` the modeled optical water path. In the source, the pure-water absorption coefficients are multiplied by four. Red-channel transmission falls faster than blue in this construction.

The path is **camera-to-object water distance**, not automatically the vehicle's depth below the surface. The code operates on ordinary encoded RGB values, without a calibrated camera-radiance model. It does not reproduce refraction, bubbles, motion blur, spatially varying illumination or the complete scattering of a real pool. This is a controlled image stress test—not evidence that these photographs were taken underwater.

| water path | 0 m | 1 m | 2 m | 3 m | 5 m | 8 m |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| AP@0.5, validation | 0.405 | 0.538 | 0.372 | 0.224 | 0.160 | 0.109 |

The one-metre result exceeds the dry result on these ten images. That can happen when a transform changes score ordering and box decisions; it is not evidence that adding water generally improves this detector. There are no uncertainty bounds here, and this small collection is not a substitute for independently recorded underwater scenes.

## What I would change—and what remains unproved

The repository includes `pipeline.fixed.config`: a lower candidate learning rate, shorter warm-up, a bounded training schedule, single-device replica settings, relative paths and additional padding and colour augmentation. **The model has not been retrained with that file. Every detector score above belongs to the original exported model.**

The validation set can guide a new experiment, but repeated tuning on it would make it a development set, not a final independent test. A convincing next evaluation needs new labelled scenes, a documented split rule and a fixed comparison protocol. Grouping by recording session, environment or product source would address relationships that exact-pixel deduplication misses.

A useful comparison would hold the starting weights, split and evaluator fixed, then change the schedule or augmentation deliberately. Report localization errors, missed objects and false positives as well as AP. A smoother training curve on its own would not establish that the detector generalizes.

The original submission is still worth keeping. Labelling photographs, getting a model through training and exporting something that draws boxes were real steps in my early vision work. The error was treating that visible success as if the experiment had already asked the harder question.
